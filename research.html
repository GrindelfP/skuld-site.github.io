Research

What the library is actually being tested on, and how its answers are scored. This page describes the benchmarks; it deliberately does not publish accuracy tables, because those change with every experiment and go stale.

The question

Can a neural network compute a definite integral of a difficult multi-dimensional function more accurately, or more cheaply, than classical quadrature? Skuld exists to answer that with measurements rather than intuition, which means every accuracy claim needs an integrand whose exact value is either known or independently computed.

Two benchmarks are used, and they answer different questions. Neither substitutes for the other.

Target benchmark: a meson Feynman integral

The primary benchmark is a real integral from a particle-physics amplitude. It has no closed form; its value is obtained by nested adaptive quadrature with scipy.integrate.quad at epsabs = epsrel = 1e-9. Being the application the method exists to serve, its score is the one that matters most.

The integrand depends on three integration variables and four discrete parameters (a, b, m, n). The variables are mapped onto the unit cube [0,1]³ by two substitutions:

  • A Korobov map on u₁ and u₂, x = u²(3 − 2u), whose Jacobian 6u(1−u) vanishes at both endpoints and so concentrates sampling where the integral actually lives. The Feynman parameters are then α₁ = x₁ and α₂ = (1 − x₁) x₂.
  • A logarithmic substitution on u₃, t = exp(L u₃) − 1 with L = log(1 + 80), which stretches [0,1] to the [0, 80] interval where the damping factor exp(−t D − t R²/(1+t)) dies out.

The remaining structure is what makes the integral hard: a factor α₁a α₂b that is non-smooth whenever the exponent is non-zero, multiplied by tm / (1 + t)n, which is sharply peaked near t = 0.

The two exponents a and b are what separate these sets: a zero exponent gives a smooth integrand, a one gives a cusp at the boundary of the simplex. Accuracy is reported per set, never averaged away.

Characterisation benchmark: the Genz test functions

A result on the meson integral says nothing about how a method behaves in general, because that integrand has one shape and one set of difficulties. To measure the method rather than the application, Skuld is also evaluated on the standard Genz test integrands, each of which has a closed-form exact integral in arbitrary dimension.

These exact values are themselves verified before use, against both scipy.integrate.cubature and arbitrary-precision mpmath — on canonical parameters and on deliberately asymmetric ones, since a broadcasting bug is invisible when every parameter happens to be equal.

Two of the five are traps worth knowing about, and both behave the same way for every method:

Oscillatory has an exact value near zero at the canonical parameters, so its relative accuracy looks terrible even when the absolute error is negligible. That is a property of the function, not a ranking signal.

Corner peak integrates to 1/24 in three dimensions. An absolute error that looks excellent can still be a large relative error, which is the whole argument for scoring on relative digits.

How accuracy is measured

Every number reported anywhere in this project is correct digits, defined as 

correct_digits = -log10(|estimate - exact| / |exact|)

always relative, never absolute. The exact values across the Genz set span 4×10−2 to 1, so an absolute metric would rank methods by the magnitude of the answer rather than by their quality.

Results are reported three ways, and the third matters as much as the first:

  • Per-integral digits. Each parameter set's own score is recorded, so the full distribution is always available and never summarised away.
  • Min, mean and max across parameter sets. The minimum shows the worst case; the mean alone would hide a method that is excellent on seven integrals and useless on the eighth.
  • Per-seed figures. A single run is an upper bound, not a measurement. Rankings that matter are computed over several seeds, because seed-to-seed variance has proved comparable to the difference between configurations.

Experimental practice

The repository separates library from research: architectures live in the installable package, and every experiment is a standalone script under research/experiments/ that writes to a mirrored results directory. Results are never written by hand into an experiment's source folder.

Negative results are kept, not deleted. Several plausible ideas have been tried and measured as not helping — importance sampling of the training data, training on the antiderivative directly, corner-focused sampling, a polynomial-chaos basis instead of a network — and the reasons they failed are recorded alongside the results, because a method's failure modes are as informative as its successes.

The experiment scripts, their results and the analysis reports are all in the repository.
